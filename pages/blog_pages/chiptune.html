Chiptune and Early Computer Music

Introduction

It is hard to imagine in today’s world of high-definition audio the humble origins of computer music. From the introduction of home computing in the 1960s, early computers lacked any audio capability other than simple beeping. It wasn’t until the introduction of computers with dedicated sound chips (hence, “chip” tune) that composing music on computers became a possibility. Much of this music was written for video games, at the a rising form of entertainment both in the arcade and at home, which is why it is so synonymous with the medium today. More of a musical “style” than a genre, chiptune, or "8-bit music," refers to music created using 1970s and 80s computer sound chips. However, in the modern day, its borders have blurred to suggest any form of artistic playfulness with early computer audio technology, and, much like jazz, a culture defined by its history.

The 8-bit era

The 8-bit computing era brought with it the iconic Nintendo GameBoy and the world’s highest-selling computer model, the Commodore 64, but more importantly, both came with dedicated sound chips - and thus marked the beginning of the history of computer music.

In essence, all electronic sounds are produced from four “waveforms”: the sine, square, triangle, and saw wave. The shape of the wave defines the timbre of the sound, the sine wave being the softest, the square wave the brightest, while the saw “Sawtooth” wave is richer, buzzier and is often used for leads and pads. The novelty of audio technology in the 8-bit era meant music was composed using some basic form of the four waveforms and explains why we associate the sounds of waveforms with early video games - think of Koji Kondo’s iconic soundtrack to the original Super Mario Bros (1985).

As well as the distinctive sound, music was also harmonically and texturally very simple, such as the four-note chromatic bass notes of the arcade game Space Invaders (1978) (the 4-note loop actually speeds up as the game intensifies).

These sound chips had only 3 channels, meaning only three notes could be played at once. Composers struggled to incorporate chords  (as a basic triad would take up all three channels and leave no place for a melody!), and thus to give the acoustic impression of a chord, composers would resort to rapid arpeggiation of the triad, which has serendipitously become the most unique, recognisable feature of the style. Early figures such as Martin Galway, Rob Hubbard and Ben Daglish are so admired to this day because they were able to create their unique musical voice with such little room for creative freedom. Galway was known for thick, expansive textures (*Wizball Title Theme*, 1987), Hubbard was a master of percussion and syncopation (Saboteur II, 1987), and Daglish’s simple melodies and thin accompaniment (Green Beret: Loading Theme, 1985) revealed a more mellow side of the medium.

Yet to compose on the Gameboy or C64 was a two-part problem. Not only musical ability, but it meant one had to manipulate sound hardware directly, program your own music playback softwares (as the technology was so new that no commercial softwares existed!), and thus required a deep understanding of the computer system itself. It isn’t surprising to learn that all the composers mentioned above were programmers first, musicians second - and the need for such cross-disciplinary skills meant it wasn’t a practical nor attractive option for music composition at all…until the introduction of the tracker.

The 16-bit era: trackers and the demoscene

It is 1984 and we have reached the dawn of the 16-bit. New releases include the Apple Macintosh, the Atari ST, and the Commodore Amiga comes with a new 4-channel sound chip nicknamed Paula. Karsten Obarski, aspiring German computer musician, is commissioned by a friend to write a soundtrack for their game on the new Commodore Amiga. Taking inspiration from the spreadsheet-like layout SoundMonitor created by older composer Chris Huelsbeck, Obarski writes his own playback software to create the composition. Named The Ultimate Soundtracker, it was actually the first of its kind: the very first tracker.

 

Songs were divided into “patterns”, with 64 rows of notes each, which were sections of music which could be repeated and rearranged to create a composition. The music for each pattern was contained in columns corresponding to four channels: Melody, accompaniment, bass, and percussion (although these were only suggestions), and note data was written in rows of 4-digit hexadecimals in each column (thus the spreadsheet-like interface). You could specify the pitch, octave, instrument, and parameter - such as portamento or vibrato - for every note in the piece. Obarski released it for commercial sale in 1987, his 126 sample disk of synthesised sounds proving to be a great starting point for musicians. The Ultimate Soundtracker, though complicated and temperamental, exposed the potential of the Amiga Paula, inspired many computer enthusiasts to make music, and still stands as a pioneer of sound sequencer technology today.

One subculture that particularly thrived from the introduction of the tracker was demoscene. A largely European phenomenon, the underground subculture emerged, like chiptune, due to the home computer revolution. Demoscene originated as an extensive network to distribute pirated computer software. Exchanges would take place through disk swapping, copying, or even car boot sales. This was a time before the World Wide Web when internet access was uncommon. Any attempted copyright legislation would prove useless against specialist programmers - “crackers” as they called themselves, who would form “cracking groups” which would work together to pirate and distribute software, especially video games. To distinguish themselves from other groups, cracking groups would play short chiptune intros, “cracktros”, at the start of their pirated game to label it as theirs.

Eventually, some groups put together longer, stand-alone productions called demos - hence, “demoscene”. These were audiovisual programs that combined tracker music with computer art and provided a canvas for coders, artists, and musicians to showcase their technical and artistic excellence. Although being a rather obscure art form, tracker chiptune music from the demoscene continued to influence video game music for decades and demonstrated the capacity for computer music to exist as a legitimate form of art just as acoustic music did.

Contemporary chiptune

Around the 1990s, chiptune began to decline with the introduction of the CD-ROM, effectively outcompeting clunky and difficult sound chip technology. It only returned to mainstream popularity in the mid 2000s due to bitpop, a subgenre of chiptune which incorporates chiptune sounds to mainstream pop, popularised by J-pop girl group Perfume (Game, 2007) and several other Japanese artists combining chiptune to synth pop and electro house. 

Modern chiptune is very loosely defined. Music that “sounds like” it could have been written in the sound chip era can be classified as chiptune. It is a style defined by the way it is created rather than the music itself; and because such method of creation is now made redundant due to modern audio technology, it is a declining style.

Nonetheless, chiptune has returned to 21st century gaming, powered by a nostalgia-driven demand for retro style video games, either fully intact or combined with modern genres to serve as evocative soundtracks to titles such as Shovel Knight (2016), and Undertale (2015), Stardew Valley (2016) and Deltarune (2018). 

Modern chip artists create authentic music using modern trackers such as Renoise or Famitracker, which imitate the sound produced on retro computers. There are also audio softwares for newer game consoles including the Game Boy Advance and Nintendo DS, which transform both devices into synthesisers. More mainstream artists include American four-piece band Anamanaguchi, who combine 8-bit chiptune with traditional band instrumentation to produce an intensely emotional digital sound. (Scott Pilgrim vs. the World: The Game OST, 2010 and Summer Singles, 2021). Disasterpiece is another American artist, who combines chiptune with ambient and experimental rock (Hyper Light Drifter OST, 2016).

So why are chip artists still drawn to the scene? Surprisingly, nostalgia may not be the only reason. On modern DAWs, composers are faced with thousands of instrument and timbral choices. Sometimes too much choice can be limiting, says Kenneth McAlpine, chiptune artist and professor at Melbourne University. Booting up a Gameboy, “the sound palette is what it is. You’ve got no option but to dive in and create, and there’s something beautifully immediate about that.”

In that sense, the style is somewhat defined by the limitations of technology and finding creative solutions around them. Much of the appeal of chiptune is that composers were able to write such memorable tracks out of the hardware constraints they were bound to; acknowledging the limits of the technology and breaking them. The history of computer music is a story of unyielding innovation, experimentation, and creativity; its legacy is chiptune, the enduring cultural icon of the 70s and 80s.
